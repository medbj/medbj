A difficult week can become your normal before you have time to notice it. You keep meeting demands, while things that usually feel manageable start to feel harder.

Noticing a change is not the same as diagnosing it. It is a chance to ask what is happening and what kind of support might be useful.

## Compare with your own usual pattern

You might notice that you are less patient, finding decisions harder, or avoiding messages. Perhaps you have stopped making time for something you enjoy. These experiences can have many causes. They do not tell you by themselves whether stress is the explanation.

Rather than checking yourself against someone else's list, ask: “What has changed for me?” You could write a few notes about when you notice it and what else is happening.

## Look at demands and resources

Think about both sides of the situation. What are you being asked to do? What time, money, energy, or help do you have available?

If demands exceed what you can manage, a personal routine may only be part of the response. You may need a clearer priority, fewer tasks, or access to practical support. You are allowed to name those needs even if a solution is not immediately available.

## Choose one useful conversation

You could tell someone you trust what has become difficult. A specific request may be easier to act on than “I need less stress.” Try “Could you help with this appointment?” or “Which of these tasks is most urgent?”

Only have a conversation if it feels safe. Where work or home is unsafe, local advice and support services may be more appropriate.

## Ask about changes that concern you

If changes persist, worry you, or affect daily life, seek advice from a qualified health professional. Physical symptoms also deserve medical advice rather than an assumption that stress is the cause.

Your notes can help explain the pattern. They do not have to be complete to be useful.
